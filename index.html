And the reweigh is the cheap part. Of the 167,118 weight citations written in 2025, 89,883 were on an axle and 27,331 on gross — 3.3 axle tickets for every one on gross. What gets written up is the spread, not the total, and the spread is what sliding fixes.

Not a rule of thumb

"About 400 pounds a hole" is a guess. The real number depends on your trailer: the weight the tandems carry, the hole spacing on the rail, and the kingpin-to-tandem distance. AxleSet takes your rig's measurements once and works every hole and every fifth-wheel notch from the geometry — then shows you what happens at each one, so you can see why the answer is what it is.

The checks the other calculators skip

  • Federal Bridge Formula (23 CFR 658.17) on every group of consecutive axles, including the 36-foot two-tandem exception everywhere it applies (New Mexico's own bridge law governs there instead). Verified cell by cell against the published table.
  • Kingpin-to-rear-axle rules — California's 40 feet and the other states that have one, including the ones that measure differently. Some of those statutes tie the rule to the length of the semitrailer; put your trailer's length in the rig profile and AxleSet judges it the way each one writes it: on a trailer the statute never covered it says the rule does not apply, not that you are legal. Where the law makes no distinction by length, it is judged with any trailer.
  • All 50 states and the District of Columbia: single, tandem, gross, bridge, and the fine schedule, each read on the state's own statute or DOT page — or on an archived copy of that official page — and cited. Every state screen says which one it was and the date it was read; nothing is guessed, and a fine table that is not on an official source is not shown at all. Where a state's own servers stayed shut and the text could only be read on a legal publisher, the state screen says so in as many words.
  • Interstate vs. state highway where the limits differ — and where a state writes a lower limit for a class of road (a Georgia county road, a Wisconsin Class B road, Kentucky's AA and A, a gravel road in Minnesota), that road class is a choice of its own on the state screen, judged with its own limit and its own citation.
  • The APU allowance as each law writes it: the federal 550 lb on the Interstate, 400 lb where the state writes that figure, and none at all where its law writes none — and the screen tells you which of the three you are getting.
  • Two readings, where the law has two. Where a state's own number is lower than the federal rule, or a 1956 grandfather figure has no statute to point to, the verdict uses the number that cannot get you a ticket and shows the other reading beside it, with the rule it comes from.
