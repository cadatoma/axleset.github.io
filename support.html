How do I count the holes?

From where your tandems are right now. "3 holes forward" means three holes toward the cab from the hole they are in today. "Back" is away from the cab. The fifth wheel works the same way: "forward" is toward the front of the tractor.

Why is my "pounds per hole" different from the number my trainer gave me?

Because it depends on your trailer. Weight moved per hole equals the trailer-axle weight times the hole spacing, divided by the kingpin-to-tandem distance. A 53-foot van at 40 feet with 6-inch holes moves about 420 lb a hole; with 4-inch holes, about 280. AxleSet calculates it for the trailer in your rig profile. That is why the profile matters.

I don't have a tape measure. How do I get the kingpin-to-tandem distance?

In My rig, tap Calibrate from two weighings. Weigh, slide the tandems a known number of holes, weigh again, and type both trailer-axle readings. AxleSet works out the distance from the two numbers.

It says "Sliding won't fix this." What now?

AxleSet tried every hole and every fifth-wheel notch you told it you have, and none of them gets every axle group under its limit. The card shows the closest it could get and what is still over. That is the moment to rework the load or drop weight — not to reweigh.

My state has county roads and designated routes. Which one does AxleSet use?

The one you pick. Most states have two sets of numbers, Interstate and everything else. Some states write a lower limit for a class of road as well — a county road in Georgia, a Class B road in Wisconsin, Kentucky's AA and A routes, a gravel road in Minnesota, a town highway in Vermont. In those states the road class is another choice on the state screen, with its own limit and its own statute cited, and the verdict is worked with that class. Mississippi has two of them on top of its state-highway numbers: the Blue Line routes, and everything the state has not designated. If you do not know which class you are on, pick the lower one: it is the one that can get you the ticket.

My state screen shows no gross limit. Is something missing?

No. A few states write axle limits and a bridge rule but no gross cap at all off the Interstate, and there AxleSet judges you by the axles and the bridge formula and says on the screen that the statute sets no gross cap — it does not invent 80,000 to fill the hole. A few go the other way: Maine, off the Interstate, lets a six-axle rig reach 100,000 lb when it meets the conditions its statute sets, and the screen quotes them. Either way the number on the screen is the one in that state's law, with its citation.

Why does AxleSet ask how long my trailer is?

Because the kingpin-to-rear-axle rule does not exist for every trailer. In some states the statute ties it to the length of the semitrailer — mostly to trailers longer than 48 feet, and in a couple of them only to the longest ones. With a shorter trailer AxleSet says the rule does not apply in that state instead of calling you legal under a rule that was never yours; where the law makes no distinction by length, the rule is judged with any trailer. The length lives in My rig, one per trailer, and it starts at 53 feet.

Why does it ask whether my drive group has a lift axle?

Because in Alaska it is worth 3,000 lb. The state writes two bridge formulas, and the one for a drive group with a lift axle in it does not carry the extra 3,000 lb that the other one does. AxleSet assumes you have one until you say otherwise, which is the strict side, and the answer lives in My rig next to the trailer length.

My state screen says there is no APU allowance. Is that right?

It is what the statute says. The federal 550 lb allowance is written for the Interstate. Off the Interstate each state decides: many keep the same 550 lb, some write 400 lb — and in Oklahoma that 400 lb lifts the bridge-formula gross only, not the axle or tandem limits. Where a state's law writes no allowance at all, AxleSet counts none and tells you so on the screen: in practice many scales still give 400 lb, but no statute obliges them to, so we do not count weight you may not have. Careful with the charts that quote a figure for the fine: some states write their credit only into how the penalty is worked out, and that is not room to load.

Why do I see two numbers for the same limit?

Because two rules can be read on the same load, and we do not hide either. Where a state's own statute is lower than the federal rule on the Interstate, or where a state applies an old 1956 figure that no statute we can read backs up, AxleSet gives the verdict with the number that cannot get you a ticket and shows the other reading right beside it, with the rule it comes from. You are the one on the road: you decide what to do with the second number.

Does "LEGAL" include a state's tolerance?

No. AxleSet judges every weight to the legal limit. Some states' statutes grant a tolerance as a right off the Interstate (Vermont on it too). On those roads an option under the strategies adds only the tolerance that road's law grants and shows its condition, and the card says when a position is legal only because of it. Where a state grants none, the option does not appear. The ticket from a certified scale is what counts.

Where do the state numbers come from, and why does a fine table say "not verified"?

Every limit comes from that jurisdiction's own statute or DOT publication — the District of Columbia's, from its own regulations — or from an archived copy of that official page. The state screen names the source, says which of the two it was, and gives the date it was read. Five states publish their fine schedule only where we could not confirm it officially; there the fines block says "not verified" and shows no dollar figures, while the weight limits above it are unaffected.

Two official sources disagree. Which one does AxleSet use?

The lower number, and it tells you. For example, one federal example and one state table round the same bridge-formula case 500 lb apart; AxleSet shows the lower figure and flags the tie.

What does an overweight ticket actually cost?

More than the number in the statute. In 2025, 167,118 weight citations were written in the United States: 89,883 on an axle against 27,331 on gross — 3.3 axle tickets for every one on gross, which is why a load that grosses fine can still cost you. And the base fine is rarely the bill: one California county court's published 2026 bail schedule turns a $20 statutory fine into $158, and a $175 one into $808; past 4,500 lb of excess, California stops calling it an infraction. Alabama told its own legislators that one truck in five inspected gets a citation and that half of those citations are for weight. AxleSet shows you the state's fine schedule with the statute cited, where the state publishes one — what you pay is decided at the roadside and in that court, not here.

Does AxleSet need internet?

No. The state limits, the fine schedules and the CAT Scale list are built in, and the ticket is read on the phone. The one exception is the optional iCloud backup of rigs and tickets: it uses your own Apple account, and only if you turn it on. That switch only stays on if the first copy really goes up — with no iCloud account signed in on the phone, AxleSet turns it back off and says so, instead of letting you believe there is a copy.

Is this legal advice?

No. AxleSet is a reference and a calculator. Laws change, permits change things, and the officer's table wins at the roadside. When in doubt, check with the state.
